It was cold and nearly dark on the last evening of the old year, and the snow was falling fast and furious.
Her roamed through it with a bare head and naked feet, looking as poor as a church mouse.
It's true she had on a pair of slippers she had once given her when she left her, but they were as much use to her as a chocolate teapot.
The slippers owned by her were so large that he had lost them while running across it to avoid two carriages that were rolling along at a terrible rate.
One of the slippers she could not find, and she seized upon the other, saying that he could use it as a home for his little pet mouse.
So with her naked feet, which were quite red and blue with the cold, her went on, wondering how she would get through this dreadful night.
She approached an old hovel she had used as a shelter a few times before, and found the entrance was locked.
Tough luck for her! This day was even worse than the others.
No Good Samaritan had given her even a penny.
And she had no luck selling any matches that she carried in the old apron she had given her.
She, she looked the picture of misery.
On her long, fair hair, which hung in curls on her shoulders, the snowflakes kept landing, but she did not notice them as she shivered along.
She was losing strength and beginning to doubt she could weather this storm.
It was New Year's, and she asked herself why, as lights shone from every window, and the savory smell of roast goose crept out on it, she was outside all alone.
Exhausted, she finally sat down in a corner behind the little bakery.
She put a rag she had found over her legs to cover herself, but she could not keep off the cold.
She, dared not go home, for she had sold no matches.
He had been mean and moody ever since she had gone to meet her Maker.
He would certainly beat her, as he has done several times in the weeks past, because she had not earned any money today.
Besides, it was almost as cold at it as outside, for they had only a patchy roof to cover them.
Her little hands were almost frozen solid so she decided that it might be of some good.
'Scratch!' sounded it as she drew it out.
How it sputtered as it burnt! It gave her.
Why had no one wanted to buy even one match? It, it was really.
It seemed to her that she was sitting by a large iron stove, with them and a brass ornament.
How it burned! She was pleased and it actually seemed so warm that she stretched out her as if to warm them, when suddenly it went out.
It was it.
It burst into it, and lo and behold, she was transported from her to it.
She heard the birds chirping and she was surrounded by wildflowers and birches as white as snow.
It hid it in the ground and scurried off into the underbrush.
She was curious, so she dug it up to see what it was, but then she forgot to put it back.
It came back and looked in its hiding place, but it was nowhere to be found.
Then it went out, and it wasn't it that remained before her eyes.
It was only it.
She looked despondently into the sky that was now fully darkened and suddenly she saw it fall, leaving behind it a bright streak of fire.
'Someone is dying,' thought she.
It was she, the one who had loved her the most, and the one who she used to visit every Sunday, who had told her that when a star falls, a soul leaves this earth.
It was her last match that she now rubbed on the wall and the light again shone round her.
She in the brightness stood, loving in her appearance.
She understood she would soon disappear, but she tried to play for time.
'She,' cried she, 'O take me with you! I know you will go away when the match burns out!' She decided to light the whole bundle of matches, in order to keep her there.
She saw the matches glowed with a light that was brighter than the day, and she had never appeared so large or so beautiful as she did in the light of the little matches.
She then took her in her arms, and they both flew upwards in brightness and joy far above the earth, where there was neither cold nor hunger, and her felt safe and peaceful.
In the dawn of the morning there lay she, not frowning, but smiling and with a pale face leaning against the wall, frozen and stone dead.
'She tried to warm herself,' said some.
They were asking their parents why no one helped her.
They wished they could have helped her, but their good intentions were a day late and a dollar short.
Other people suspected she had fallen asleep and frozen, but no one imagined what beautiful things she had seen, nor into what glory she had entered with her, on New Year's day.
